Ranking Signal Analysis: Predicting Content Decline from Search Performance Data

Muhammad Haroon Rana  ·  September 2026

Question. Which content and search signals are associated with a page's search performance declining, and can a trained model prioritize pages for review better than a hand-built rule? Method. Using a 30,000-row dataset of anonymized page-level search metrics across 32 clients, I built a transparent weighted baseline rule, then trained Logistic Regression and Random Forest classifiers to predict decline, validated with a client-grouped train/test split so no client's pages appeared in both sides of the comparison. Result. The Random Forest model (ROC-AUC 0.566) outperformed Logistic Regression (0.541) and the hand-built baseline (0.498, near chance), with average search position emerging as the dominant predictive signal. Implication. A simple trained model meaningfully improves on hand-built prioritization rules for this task, though the improvement is modest and should inform triage, not replace human review. Scope. This work is decision-support and directional, not causal, and has not been validated across time — only across unseen clients.

1. Introduction and problem statement

Content teams need to decide which pages to spend limited review time on. This project asks: which observed content and search signals are associated with better or worse search rankings, and can that relationship be used to prioritize pages before they decline further?

Decision this informs: which webpages should be reviewed and potentially refreshed. Who acts on it: a content team prioritizing a review queue. Cost of a wrong recommendation: time and effort spent improving pages that did not actually need it, or missing pages that did.

This paper reports observed, directional relationships. It does not claim to prove causation, and it does not predict how any specific search engine's ranking algorithm works.

2. Data

The dataset is a starter release from the FlyRank ML Internship: content_refresh_anonymized.csv, 30,000 rows and 44 columns, one row per pseudonymized content item across 32 clients, with trailing-90-day performance metrics (search volume, click-through rate, average position, engagement rate, scroll rate, and related fields).

Two columns, trend_direction and trend_pct, directly determine the outcome label and were therefore excluded from the model's input features to prevent label leakage. content_id and client_id are pseudonyms used only for grouping and splitting, never as model inputs. No client names, URLs, or private queries appear anywhere in this paper or its underlying notebooks.

3. Methodology

3.1 Baseline

The baseline is a transparent, hand-built rule combining four weighted components: visibility (40%), freshness risk (30%), position opportunity (25%), and content depth gap (5%), producing a 0–1 refresh-priority score. It involves no learning — the weights were chosen by hand, not fit to data.

3.2 Label and features

The target label, is_declining_label, is 1 when a page's observed trend direction is downward. Ten numeric signals were used as model inputs: search volume, competition, CPC, word count, character count, click-through rate, average position, engagement rate, scroll rate, and AI-traffic percentage.

3.3 Validation design

Rows were split by client_id using a grouped 80/20 train-test split, ensuring no client's pages appeared in both the training and test sets — a more honest test of generalization than a random row-level split, which would let the model partially memorize client-specific patterns.

3.4 Models trained

Logistic Regression (scaled features) and a Random Forest classifier (200 trees, max depth 6) were trained on the same split and compared against the baseline rule using ROC-AUC on the identical held-out test set.

4. Results

Model performance versus the hand-built baseline, evaluated on the same client-grouped test set:

Random Forest (0.566) outperforms Logistic Regression (0.541) and the baseline rule (0.498, effectively chance) on the identical client-grouped test set.

Error analysis on the Random Forest at a 0.5 threshold shows the model favors catching real declines over avoiding false alarms: it correctly identifies 91% of actually-declining pages (recall), but only 15% of actually-stable pages, meaning it over-flags a meaningful share of stable pages for review.

Feature importance from the Random Forest:

avg_position alone accounts for 46% of the model's decisions — more than three times any other single feature.

5. Limitations and honest framing

  • No time-based validation was performed — only a client-grouped split. The model has been shown to generalize to unseen clients, but has not been tested against future time periods, which is the more relevant real-world question for "will this page decline."
  • The model is trained on a single ~30,000-row snapshot and may not reflect newer content types, seasonal effects, or search algorithm changes since collection.
  • Findings are observed and directional. They do not establish causation, and they do not predict any specific search engine's ranking algorithm.
  • A "high risk" prediction is a probability, not a certainty, and should not be treated as a verdict on any single page in isolation.
  • The model does not account for business priority, revenue value, or legal/compliance constraints; those require human judgment on top of this output.

6. Ranked recommendations

Combining the trained model's decline-probability score with concrete page signals, pages are grouped into archetypes, each with a recommended action:

ArchetypeSignal patternRecommended action
High risk, thin contentDecline probability ≥0.6, word count under 1,200Expand and refresh
High risk, page oneDecline probability ≥0.6, ranking position 1–10Refresh, priority review
High risk, generalDecline probability ≥0.6Refresh and review
Medium riskDecline probability 0.4–0.6Monitor closely
Low riskDecline probability <0.4No action, quarterly review

Human review rules

  • Any page flagged high risk, page one must be reviewed by a human before action — these are currently well-ranked pages, where a false positive risks harming something that didn't need touching.
  • Any recommendation to remove or substantially rewrite a page requires human sign-off; the model has no signal about legal, compliance, or brand constraints.
  • Low-sample groupings (fewer than roughly 20 pages) should be reviewed manually rather than acted on in bulk.

What should not be automated

  • Content changes should never be auto-published directly from this model's output.
  • Pages should never be auto-deleted or deindexed based solely on a decline-probability score.
  • An individual page's probability should never be treated as certainty; this tool is for batch-level prioritization, not a page-by-page automatic verdict.

Monitoring and retrain triggers

  • Re-run and re-validate at least quarterly, since training data is a single snapshot.
  • If human reviewers find more than roughly 30% of "high risk" flagged pages are actually fine on inspection, that signals the model needs retraining or re-auditing.
  • A noticeable shift in the overall proportion of declining pages versus what was seen in training is a data-drift signal worth investigating before trusting the model further.
